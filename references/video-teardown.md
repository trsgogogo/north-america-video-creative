# Reference video teardown

## Access and evidence

Inspect the actual supplied video through available tools, or retrieve the provided public link when accessible. For a local video, inspect representative frames and audio/transcript with available media tools; increase sampling around transitions rather than invent unseen motion. Record exactly what was accessible: full audio/video, selected frames, transcript only, thumbnail only, caption/metrics only. Never say “watched” when only text was read. If inaccessible, request a usable video or transcript and offer a clearly limited analysis of available material.

A transcript supports language/structure analysis, not camera, acting, sound, cuts or visual pacing claims. A still image cannot establish timing or sequence. Use actual timestamps when available; otherwise label paragraph/beat positions or approximate inferred timing. Do not invent retention graphs, traffic sources, audience demographics, ad spend, revenue, or conversion.

For a set of references, log URL/file, creator if known, capture date, duration, topic, observable metrics and access level. Public views alone neither prove virality relative to the account baseline nor explain its cause. Sample both stronger and weaker comparable examples when available; note selection bias and paid/organic uncertainty.

## Teardown output

Start with the strongest actionable finding and its evidence, then use:

| Time/beat | Observed line/action | Creative function | Interpretation and confidence | Transferable lesson |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Keep observation separate from inference. Quote only brief necessary excerpts; paraphrase the rest.

Inspect these elements where evidence permits:
1. First frame and first sentence: who recognizes the situation; what benefit, tension or curiosity is promised; relationship between picture and words.
2. Attention progression: setup, reveal, escalation, demonstration, payoff; what new information each beat contributes; whether the opening promise is paid off.
3. Product integration: scene → problem → mechanism → evidence → relevance. Distinguish a visible demo from an unsupported spoken assertion.
4. Craft: shot changes, visual hierarchy, caption readability, sound and pauses. Explain their likely function, not a made-up algorithm preference.
5. Conversion: one primary CTA, relevance to the content, next-step effort, unresolved objection.
6. Dependence: personal reputation, actual results, celebrity access, production budget, trend timing or unique footage that may not transfer.

## Translate analysis into original creative

Deliver a structural skeleton in neutral terms, e.g. “show a familiar failure → reveal overlooked cause → demonstrate one fix → state the limit → invite the next step.” Do not copy distinctive wording, jokes or fabricated personal experiences.

Map each major reference element: original function | keep/change/drop | user's replacement | proof/asset needed. Preserve the mechanism only when it fits the user's product and audience. Replace culture-specific jokes with recognizable scenes; do not assume all US, Canadian and Mexican audiences share references.

Then write a complete original script and production brief using scripts.md, unless the user requested analysis only. For a single requested remake, pick the strongest format rather than automatically producing four. Explain the key creative choices in a few sentences.

## Without performance data

Use “may help because…” or “test whether…” for effect claims. Offer a falsifiable test, e.g. same body/CTA with two different openings, compared on a consistently defined early-view metric and downstream qualified actions. Do not conclude that the hook caused the reference's success.
